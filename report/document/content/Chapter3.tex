\chapter{Data Analysis}
\label{chap:data}

\section{Dataset Overview}

This project uses the \textit{Yamanishi08} benchmark for drug--target
interaction (DTI) prediction. The dataset represents known interactions as a
bipartite graph whose two node sets are drugs and target proteins. Each
observed edge denotes a known positive interaction, while the remaining
drug--target pairs are unobserved. These unobserved pairs are used to sample
negative training and test examples; they should not be interpreted as
experimentally confirmed biological negatives.

The dataset also provides 1024-dimensional Morgan fingerprints for drugs,
147-dimensional CTD descriptors for proteins, and a biomedical knowledge graph
used to construct entity embeddings. Since the main objective of this chapter
is to characterize the DTI prediction problem, the analysis focuses on the
interaction network and the two warm-start evaluation settings rather than on
the individual descriptor distributions.

The auxiliary knowledge graph contains 95,672 raw rows, corresponding to
95,579 unique triples after removing 93 duplicates. It contains 25,487 entities
and 48 relation types, and covers all 791 drugs and 989 targets in the DTI
network. Thus, graph context is available for every entity evaluated in this
project.

\section{Interaction Scarcity}

Table~\ref{tab:yamanishi_dti_stats} shows that the DTI prediction space is
extremely sparse. Only 5,127 of the 782,299 possible drug--target pairs are
observed as positive, giving a density of 0.6554\%. Equivalently, 99.3446\% of
the interaction matrix is unobserved. Consequently, the learning problem is
defined by a small set of positive observations within a much larger candidate
space.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/data_analysis/network_sparsity_summary.png}
    \caption{Sparsity, low-degree prevalence, and hub concentration in the
    Yamanishi08 DTI network.}
    \label{fig:yamanishi_sparsity_summary}

Figure~\ref{fig:yamanishi_sparsity_summary} summarizes three aspects of this
scarcity. In addition to the low matrix density, most entities have little
interaction evidence, while a relatively small group of hubs accounts for a
large fraction of all observed edges. The dataset is therefore sparse both at
the pair level and at the entity level.

\section{Degree Distribution and Imbalance}

The median degree is only two for both drugs and targets, although the maximum
degrees reach 132 and 61, respectively. This gap indicates a strongly skewed
degree distribution. In particular, 74.34\% of drugs have no more than five
known targets, and 55.01\% of targets interact with no more than two drugs.

Figure~\ref{fig:yamanishi_degree_distribution} confirms that low-degree
entities dominate both sides of the bipartite graph. The Gini coefficients in
Table~\ref{tab:yamanishi_degree_stats} further quantify this imbalance. The
top 10\% of drugs account for 57.03\% of all observed interactions, whereas the
top 10\% of targets account for 41.49\%. Evaluation results may therefore be
influenced disproportionately by well-connected entities, while predictions
for low-degree entities remain supported by much less direct evidence.

\section{Warm-Start Evaluation Settings}

The experiments use two warm-start settings: 1:1 and 1:10. In both settings,
the same positive interactions are retained, and every test drug and target
also appears in the corresponding training data. The settings differ in the
number of unobserved pairs sampled as negatives. The 1:1 setting is nearly
balanced, whereas the 1:10 setting introduces approximately ten sampled
negatives for each positive test pair.

\begin{figure}[H]

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:warm_start_comparison} and
Figure~\ref{fig:warm_start_class_balance}, changing from 1:1 to 1:10 does not
increase the amount of positive supervision. It only increases the number of
sampled negative pairs, producing a more realistic but more imbalanced ranking
problem. The 1:1 setting measures performance under balanced class exposure,
whereas the 1:10 setting places greater emphasis on rejecting non-interacting
candidates and is therefore expected to be more difficult, particularly for
precision-oriented metrics.

The data audit found no duplicate rows, no train--test pair overlap, and no
sampled-negative pair that appears in the known-positive file. The ten supplied
splits should be treated as repeated warm-start holdouts rather than as a
disjoint partition: together, their test sets contain 3,120 unique positive
pairs, and some positive pairs occur in more than one test split. Experimental
results are consequently reported as the mean and standard deviation across
the supplied splits.

\section{Implications for the Prediction Task}

The analysis establishes two central difficulties. First, positive DTI evidence
is scarce: fewer than one percent of all candidate pairs are observed, and most
drugs and targets have only a few known interactions. Second, the 1:10 setting
substantially increases class imbalance without adding positive evidence. A
successful model must therefore exploit information beyond direct interaction
frequency and remain effective when positives are rare among candidate pairs.
These properties motivate the use of knowledge graph embeddings together with
the NFM prediction component introduced in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.
